Start here

There is far too much written about AI agents, and most of it repeats the same three diagrams. This page is deliberately short. Every link was checked in October 2026, and anything we couldn't confirm was left out.

If you only have a weekend, read or watch these six, roughly in this order. The first two are vendor guides written by people who build agents for a living. They disagree on details and agree on the big thing: start simple and add autonomy only when you can measure that it helps.

Manager's lens

You don't need to read the papers to manage an agent project. You do need to read one vendor guide end to end and one standard (NIST AI RMF or ISO/IEC 42001) well enough to ask your team where their controls are. That's maybe six hours. It will change the questions you ask in every review meeting.

Things move fast

Course names, prices and documentation addresses change every few months. If a link breaks, search the publisher's site for the title. Where we say "free", we mean free to watch or read as of October 2026; some platforms charge for certificates or graded work.

Vendor guides and docs

Vendor guides are marketing as well as engineering, so read them with that in mind. Still, the best ones are unusually candid about what fails. Anthropic's tool-writing post, for instance, is mostly about how small changes to a tool's description change whether the agent uses it properly, which is something every capstone team rediscovers the hard way.

Key papers

You can run an agent programme without reading a single paper. But a handful of them explain why today's agents work the way they do, and knowing the names helps when a vendor says "we use a ReAct-style loop with reflection" and you want to know whether that means anything. Each is on arXiv and free to read. The one-liners are the manager's version.

Standards and regulation

This is the list your risk lead should know by heart. Most corporate AI policies in India borrow from the NIST framework and ISO/IEC 42001 for structure, follow the DPDP Act for personal data, and keep an eye on the EU AI Act because their customers or parent companies are in Europe. The OWASP lists are the practical security checklists your IT team will recognise.

Dates are moving

Two of these have shifting timelines. The EU changed its high-risk dates through the AI Omnibus in 2026, and the DPDP Rules phase in over 18 months from November 2025. Check the official page before you quote a date in a board paper.

Industry reports

Useful for the "why now" slide of a business case, and for a reality check. Read the methodology section before you quote a number. Most of these are surveys of what executives say, which isn't the same as what their companies measured. McKinsey's 2026 survey, for example, reports that 40% of organisations with more than $1 billion in revenue say they are scaling AI agents, up from 27% a year earlier. That's a self-report from large firms, not a census.

Key takeaways

  • Start with two vendor guides (Anthropic's and OpenAI's) and one course; that's enough to hold your own in a design review.
  • Most DeepLearning.AI, Hugging Face, Microsoft and LangChain Academy material is free to start; certificates and graded work may cost extra.
  • For governance, know NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, the OWASP lists, the DPDP Act and Rules, and the EU AI Act's current dates.
  • Check licences before you build on an "open" tool: n8n, Dify and Phoenix are source-available, not open source.
