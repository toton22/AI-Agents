The big idea

Most agent projects that fail do not fail on technology. They fail on value, cost or risk. The agent works, more or less, but nobody can say what it is worth, the bill grows, the risk team gets nervous, and the project is quietly shelved.

This is not a hunch. In June 2025 Gartner predicted that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. The same release warned about "agent washing", estimating that only about 130 of the thousands of vendors calling their products agentic actually are. And McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey found that 37% of respondents attribute at least some EBIT impact to AI, essentially unchanged from the year before. Lots of activity; much less proven value.

The fix is unglamorous management work, done before and after the build: choose use cases where agents fit, model the value with assumptions everyone can see, measure against a baseline, give every agent an owner, and govern the whole portfolio so risk is managed rather than discovered.

Manager's lens

You are the person who will be asked "was it worth it?" twelve months from now. You can only answer if you write down three things before the build starts: the baseline (how long, how costly, how error-prone the work is today), the assumptions behind the benefit, and the metric you will use to check. Everything in this module serves that conversation.

How it works

Finding the right use cases

Do not start with "where can we use agents?" Start with work. Take a role or a process and break it into tasks, then ask of each task: how often does it happen, how rule-bound is it, how much judgement does it need, what does a mistake cost, and can the data and systems be reached? This is task decomposition, and it nearly always shows that an agent should take some tasks, not the whole job.

Deal desk task at MeridianVolumeNatureAgent fit
Look up customer tier, payment history, marginEvery requestPure retrievalHigh: tools do it in seconds
Check the request against discount policyEvery requestRulesHigh, with the rule enforced in code
Draft the reply or the approval noteEvery requestWriting with contextHigh: the model's strength
Decide on exceptions above policyAbout a third of requestsJudgement, accountabilityLow: a manager decides; the agent prepares the case
Negotiate with the customer's procurement teamOccasionalRelationship, tacticsNone: human work

The pattern generalises into a sweet spot: boring, high-volume, rules-plus-judgement work. Boring means stable and well understood, so you can write a golden set. High-volume means small savings per task add up. Rules-plus-judgement means pure rules are better served by ordinary automation, while pure judgement is too risky to delegate; agents earn their keep in the middle, where they read messy inputs, apply rules, and hand a well-prepared decision to a human. OpenAI's practical guide to building agents lands in the same place: complex decisions, rules that are hard to maintain, and heavy use of unstructured data. Anthropic's advice adds the counterweight: use the simplest thing that works, which is often a single model call or a fixed workflow rather than an agent.

Score each axis with a few simple 1-to-5 questions rather than gut feel. Value: hours at stake, money at stake (leakage, revenue, penalties), and strategic importance. Feasibility: are the data and systems reachable through APIs, are the rules written down, what does an error cost, and how many systems must be integrated? The Deal Desk lands top-right because the rules are written, the data sits in two systems, and a wrong answer is caught by an approval step.

The ROI model

An honest agent ROI model has six moving parts. On the benefit side: hours saved, cycle time (faster answers, sometimes worth more than the hours), quality (fewer errors, less leakage, better compliance) and revenue uplift. On the cost side: run cost (models, hosting, tools, the people who own and review it), build cost and change cost (training, process redesign, communication). Here it is for the Deal Desk. Every number is an assumption, labelled so you can challenge it; none is a claim about any real company.

Three lessons hide in that table. First, the realisation assumption swings everything; "hours saved" is not cash unless the hours go somewhere useful, so say where they go (more customer calls, faster quotes) and track it. Second, the quality benefit (leakage) is what makes the base case work; agents that enforce a policy often pay through control, not speed. Third, revenue uplift is not in the base case at all. Faster quotes probably win some deals, but count that as upside and measure it, rather than putting it in the business case as fact.

The operating model

Someone has to own each agent after the launch party. Companies that scale agents settle on a small set of roles, usually around a central team.

  • The AI Centre of Excellence (CoE) is a small central team. It runs the shared platform, sets standards (eval, guardrail and deployment checklists from M10 to M12), keeps the agent registry, and coaches business teams. It should not build every agent itself, or it becomes a bottleneck.
  • The business sponsor owns the value case and is accountable for the benefit actually landing. For the Deal Desk, Meridian's head of sales.
  • The product owner manages the backlog and adoption: which requests to support next, what users complain about.
  • The agent owner runs one agent day to day: watches the dashboard, triages incidents, keeps the golden set current when policy changes. Often a sales-ops analyst with part of their week set aside.
  • Risk and compliance sets the risk tier, approves launches of higher-tier agents, and audits.
  • IT and security own identity, hosting, data access and integration.
  • Human reviewers are the managers and specialists in the loop. It is a real role, so give it time, training and its own metrics.

Agent governance

Governance is how a company knows what agents it has, how risky each is, who approved it, and whether it still behaves. Five mechanisms cover most of it.

1. An inventory or registry. You cannot govern what you cannot list. Every agent, including the no-code ones built in a department, gets an entry.

2. Risk tiering. Not every agent needs a committee. Tier by impact and autonomy, and scale the controls to match.

TierTypical agentControls
Tier 1: lowInternal Q&A over public or non-sensitive documents; drafts that a person always editsRegistry entry, standard checklist, owner sign-off
Tier 2: mediumActs in business systems within limits, touches confidential data (the Deal Desk)Golden set and red-team evidence, CoE review, human approval above thresholds, quarterly review
Tier 3: highAffects individuals' rights or money (hiring, credit, claims), customer-facing at scale, or high autonomyApproval board, impact assessment, bias testing, legal review, EU AI Act and DPDP checks, ongoing audit

3. An approval board for Tier 3 and for exceptions: a small group from the business, risk, legal, IT and the CoE that meets on a fixed cadence and decides quickly. Its job is a documented yes, no or "yes with conditions", not endless review.

4. Model and agent cards. A one-to-two page document per agent saying what it does, what it must not do, which models and data it uses, known limitations, eval results and the human checkpoints. Write it for a reader in audit or legal, not for engineers.

5. Audit. Periodic checks that the agent still matches its card: sample the logs, re-run the golden set, confirm permissions have not crept. If you want an external frame, NIST's AI RMF (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) gives you the vocabulary, and ISO/IEC 42001 gives you a certifiable management system; M11 covers both alongside the EU AI Act and India's DPDP rules.

Change management and the workforce

Here's the bit business cases usually skip. People decide whether an agent succeeds, and the people most affected often hear about it last.

Redesign the job as well as the task. If the deal-desk analyst no longer checks policy by hand, what does the role become? At Meridian a sensible answer is: handle the exceptions the agent escalates, own the golden set, analyse discount patterns, coach reps on pricing. That is a better job, but only if someone designs it and says so.

Reskill deliberately. Reviewers need training on what agents get wrong and how to check them. Agent owners need basic skills in evals, dashboards and prompt changes. Budget time for it in the change cost; it is not free.

Tell people early and honestly. Staff will assume the worst if you say nothing. Explain what the agent will do, what it will not, what happens to roles, and how they can raise problems. If no jobs are being cut, say so; if some are changing, say how. A vague message invites quiet resistance and workarounds.

Make "human in the loop" a real role. Approving an agent's escalation is a decision with accountability. Give reviewers clear criteria, a reasonable volume, and feedback on their own accuracy. A reviewer who rubber-stamps 200 approvals a day is not a control.

Procurement checklist for agent vendors

  • Show it working on our data and our golden set, not a scripted demo
  • What exactly is agentic here: which decisions and tool calls does it make on its own?
  • Which models are used, where are they hosted, and can we choose or pin versions?
  • Where are prompts, logs, memory and traces stored; is India-region hosting available?
  • Data processing agreement, sub-processor list, DPDP and breach-notification commitments
  • Is our data used for training? Default and contractual position
  • Identity: SSO support, on-behalf-of access, role-based permissions
  • Guardrails: tool allow-lists, approval steps, spend and rate limits we control
  • Observability: can we export traces and logs, ideally via OpenTelemetry?
  • Evaluation: can we run our own test sets and see results per release?
  • Pricing model at 10× our pilot volume; caps and alerts
  • Certifications and frameworks (ISO/IEC 27001, ISO/IEC 42001, SOC 2) and evidence
  • Exit: data export, and what happens to our agents if we leave

Hands-on: build the case

You will build the Deal Desk ROI model with sensitivity cases, then fill in a one-page business case. Pick the path that suits you; the numbers are the same.

  1. Make an Assumptions tab

    One row per assumption from B1 to C3 above: a label, a value, a source ("sales ops estimate", "finance", "vendor quote") and an owner. Name each value cell (for example Requests, MinBefore, Realisation) so formulas read like sentences.

  2. Compute monthly benefits

    Time value: =Requests*(MinBefore-MinAfter)/60*CostPerHour*Realisation. Leakage avoided: =Requests*LeakShare*AvgOrder*LeakPoints*LeakPrevented.

  3. Lay out 36 months

    Columns for months 1 to 36. Benefit row: full benefit times 0.5 in months 1 to 3, times 1 after. Run cost row: =-RunCost. Net row: benefit plus run cost. Put the one-time cost in a month-0 column.

  4. Add the summary

    Cumulative cash row; payback is the first month the cumulative turns positive (=MATCH(TRUE, INDEX(CumRow>=0,0), 0)). Year-1 net is the sum of months 1 to 12 plus month 0. Three-year ROI is total net divided by total cost.

  5. Add scenarios

    Copy the assumptions into Base, Pessimistic and Optimistic columns and switch between them with a dropdown, or use Excel's Scenario Manager. Show all three to decision-makers, always.

No packages needed. Save as roi_model.py and run python roi_model.py.

"""Deal Desk agent ROI model. All inputs are assumptions: change them, not the formulas."""

A = {
    "requests_per_month": 40 * 3 * 22,   # reps x requests a day x working days
    "minutes_before": 25,                # rep + analyst effort per request today
    "minutes_after": 5,                  # rep reviews the agent's draft
    "cost_per_hour": 600,                # loaded cost of that time, Rs
    "realisation": 0.50,                 # share of saved time that turns into value
    "leak_share": 0.02,                  # requests approved above policy today
    "avg_order": 150_000,                # Rs per order with a discount request
    "leak_points": 0.02,                 # average excess discount on those orders
    "leak_prevented": 0.75,              # share of leakage the agent stops
    "run_cost_month": 36_280 + 50_000 + 20_000,  # platform (M12) + owner time + upkeep
    "one_time": 18_00_000 + 6_00_000 + 4_00_000,  # build + integration + change
    "pilot_months": 3,                   # months at half benefit while ramping
}


def monthly_benefit(a):
    hours = a["requests_per_month"] * (a["minutes_before"] - a["minutes_after"]) / 60
    time_value = hours * a["cost_per_hour"] * a["realisation"]
    leakage = (a["requests_per_month"] * a["leak_share"] * a["avg_order"]
               * a["leak_points"] * a["leak_prevented"])
    return time_value, leakage


def cash_flows(a, months=36):
    time_value, leakage = monthly_benefit(a)
    flows = []
    for m in range(1, months + 1):
        ramp = 0.5 if m <= a["pilot_months"] else 1.0
        flows.append((time_value + leakage) * ramp - a["run_cost_month"])
    return flows


def summary(a, label):
    flows = cash_flows(a)
    cum, payback = -a["one_time"], None
    for m, f in enumerate(flows, start=1):
        cum += f
        if payback is None and cum >= 0:
            payback = m
    year1 = sum(flows[:12]) - a["one_time"]
    total_cost = a["one_time"] + a["run_cost_month"] * 36
    roi3 = (sum(flows) - a["one_time"]) / total_cost
    lakh = lambda x: f"Rs {x / 1e5:,.1f} lakh"
    print(f"{label:<14} year-1 net {lakh(year1):>17}   3-yr ROI {roi3:6.0%}   "
          f"payback month {payback or 'none in 3 yrs'}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    t, l = monthly_benefit(A)
    print(f"Monthly benefit at full run: time Rs {t:,.0f} + leakage Rs {l:,.0f}\n")
    summary(A, "Base case")
    summary({**A, "realisation": 0.25, "leak_prevented": 0.0}, "Pessimistic")
    summary({**A, "realisation": 0.75}, "Optimistic")

Output:

Base case      year-1 net      Rs -0.6 lakh   3-yr ROI    99%   payback month 13
Pessimistic    year-1 net     Rs -26.9 lakh   3-yr ROI   -31%   payback month none in 3 yrs
Optimistic     year-1 net      Rs 13.3 lakh   3-yr ROI   168%   payback month 9

What this does, in plain English:

  • A holds every assumption in one place, written in Indian notation (18_00_000 is ₹18 lakh). Change assumptions here, never inside the formulas.
  • monthly_benefit() turns hours saved into rupees at the realisation rate, and computes the leakage the agent stops.
  • cash_flows() builds 36 months of net cash, at half benefit during the pilot months.
  • summary() finds the payback month, the year-1 net and the three-year ROI, and prints one line per scenario.
  • The last lines run three scenarios by overriding a couple of assumptions each. Add your own, for example "integration costs double".

In the business

Case · HR

Hours saved that went nowhere

A shared-services centre deployed an HR query agent and reported thousands of hours saved in its first quarter. Finance asked where the hours had gone. Nobody knew: team size, overtime and backlog were unchanged. The second business case was rebuilt around a concrete use of the time (clearing a long-standing backlog of employee record corrections) and tracked that, which made the value visible and defensible.

Case · Finance

Control was the real return

A manufacturing group's agent for checking travel and expense claims against policy saved modest reviewer time. The bigger win, which the original case had not counted, was consistency: out-of-policy claims that used to slip through on busy days were now flagged every time. The CFO's team re-based the business case on leakage prevented and audit findings closed, and expanded the agent to supplier invoices on that basis.

Case · Customer service

The vendor demo versus the golden set

A telecom operator shortlisted three vendors for a billing-dispute agent. All three demos looked excellent. The procurement team then gave each vendor the same 200 anonymised real disputes as a golden set and scored the results. One vendor's pass rate was far below its demo impression, largely because its product could not handle the operator's bundled-plan rules. The checklist question "show it working on our data" saved a costly mistake.

Case · Supply chain

A registry found seventeen agents

A consumer-goods company set up an agent registry and asked every department to declare what it had built. The CoE expected four or five. It found seventeen, including no-code agents in procurement with write access to vendor master data and no named owner. Within a quarter, each had an owner and a risk tier, three were retired, and two were rebuilt on the shared platform with proper approvals.

Pitfalls

No baseline. If you did not measure the "before", you cannot prove the "after". Measure during the pilot at the latest.

Hours equal rupees. Saved hours only turn into value if they are redeployed or remove a cost. Use a realisation rate and say where the time goes.

Counting revenue you hope for. Faster quotes may win more deals. Put that in the upside and measure it with a comparison group, not in the base case.

Forgetting run and people costs. Model tokens are often the smallest line. Owner time, reviewer time, platform fees and upkeep are what make or break the case.

Use cases chosen by enthusiasm. The most exciting idea is rarely top-right on the matrix. Score candidates the same way and start where value and feasibility are both high.

Governance as a gate, not a service. If getting an agent approved takes six months, departments will build in the shadows. Tier the controls so low-risk agents move fast and high-risk ones get real scrutiny.

Silence with staff. An agent launched without explaining what happens to people's jobs meets resistance that no ROI model predicts.

Watch out

Be careful with headline statistics in your own business case. Survey numbers and analyst predictions describe other companies. Your board needs your baseline, your assumptions and your pilot results.

Practice

  1. Decompose a job

    Pick a role you know (accounts payable clerk, HR business partner, inside-sales rep). List eight tasks, rate each for volume, rules, judgement and error cost, and mark which an agent should take, assist with, or leave alone.

    Hint / model answer

    For an AP clerk: invoice data capture (take), three-way match (take, code-enforced), vendor queries about payment status (take), exception investigation (assist), vendor bank-detail changes (leave: fraud risk), month-end accrual judgement (assist), relationship issues with key suppliers (leave), process improvement (leave).

  2. Plot your portfolio

    Choose six candidate agent use cases for a company you know. Score each 1 to 5 on value and feasibility using the questions in this module, plot them on the matrix, and pick the first two to build.

    Hint / model answer

    Good first picks are top-right and also different in kind: one that saves time at volume and one that improves control. Avoid starting with a big bet that needs integrations you do not have.

  3. Stress-test the ROI

    Using the spreadsheet or roi_model.py, find the break-even realisation rate at which the three-year ROI is zero, keeping leakage at the base case. Then double the integration cost and repeat. What does this tell you about where to focus during the pilot?

    Hint / model answer

    With base-case leakage, three-year ROI hits zero at roughly 14% realisation; doubling integration cost (₹6 lakh more) moves that to about 18% and pushes payback from month 13 to month 15. So the case survives quite a lot of disappointment on time savings, because the leakage benefit carries it. The pilot should therefore measure two things hard: how much above-policy discounting actually stops, and whether saved time is redeployed.

  4. Write the agent card

    Write a one-page agent card for the Deal Desk for an audience of internal audit: purpose, users, tools and permissions, data, models, limits, human checkpoints, eval evidence, owner and review date.

    Hint / model answer

    Use the registry fields as headings. The parts auditors look for first: what the agent can do without a human, how that is enforced (code, not prompt), what evidence shows it works, and who is accountable.

  5. Draft the staff message

    Write the 200-word note the Head of Sales sends to the sales team and deal-desk analysts announcing the Deal Desk pilot. Cover what changes, what does not, what happens to the analysts' work, and how to give feedback.

    Hint / model answer

    Lead with the problem people already feel (slow discount answers). Be specific: the agent handles in-policy requests, managers still decide exceptions, analysts move to exceptions and pricing analysis, no roles are being cut (if true). Name the feedback channel and the date of the first review.

Key takeaways

  • Agent projects usually fail on value, cost or risk, not technology. Plan for all three before you build.
  • Decompose jobs into tasks and aim for the sweet spot: boring, high-volume, rules-plus-judgement work.
  • Build an ROI model with labelled assumptions, a realisation rate, all run and change costs, and three scenarios.
  • Track adoption, efficiency, quality and money against a measured baseline.
  • Give every agent a sponsor and an owner, register it, tier its risk, and scale controls to the tier.
  • Redesign jobs, train reviewers, and tell staff early; the human in the loop is a real role.
